# How to keep your Weber Genesis cooking like new

I'm Jeff, and I clean grills for a living all over Cincinnati, Northern Kentucky, and Dayton. More of those grills are Webers than anything else — Genesis, Genesis II, the ones with the Sear Station. There's a reason. A Weber Genesis is built to last 15 years or more if you take care of it. The grills I see struggling aren't worn out. They're just buried under a few seasons of grease and carbon nobody scraped off in time.

Here's how I'd keep yours cooking like the day you bought it.

## Flavorizer bars are the part people forget

The flavorizer bars are those angled metal strips sitting over your burners. They're the whole point of how a Weber cooks — drippings hit them, vaporize, and that's your flavor and your even heat. They're also the first thing to clog.

When they cake up, three things happen: your heat goes uneven, you get more flare-ups, and the bars themselves rust through faster. A few times a season, pull the grates and run a putty knife or a brush down each bar to knock the buildup off. If they're warped, pitted, or rusted thin, replace them — on a Genesis they're cheap and they slide right out. A fresh set of flavorizer bars can make a tired grill feel new again.

## Grates: scrape hot, deep-clean cold

The wire-brush-while-it's-hot routine is fine for day-to-day, and it's all most people ever do. But that only touches the top of the bars. The underside and the gaps fill in with hardened carbon that a brush won't move.

Once a month in grilling season, let the grates go cold and actually scrub them — both sides. Cast iron grates (what most Genesis models run) hold heat beautifully but need a light coat of oil after cleaning so they don't rust. Porcelain-coated grates clean up easier but the coating chips if you go at them with steel wool, so go gentle. The goal isn't a showroom shine. It's a clean cooking surface that doesn't transfer last month's burgers onto tonight's.

## Grease management keeps the whole thing alive

This is the one that actually kills grills, and it's the easiest to ignore. Grease runs off the flavorizer bars, down the slanted cookbox floor, through the drip hole, and into a little tray under the grill. When that path clogs — and it always clogs — grease pools in the bottom of the cookbox instead.

A cookbox full of old grease is a grease fire waiting for a windy Saturday. Check your drip tray every few cookouts and scrape the cookbox floor down to bare metal a couple times a season. On the Weber Genesis I cleaned in Mt. Lookout, the cookbox and flavorizer bars were the dramatic part of the job — the grates looked rough, but the real gunk was underneath, where nobody looks. That's the deep clean that adds years.

## Cincinnati weather is harder on your grill than you think

Our climate doesn't do a grill any favors. Humid summers, freeze-thaw winters, and that habit we all have of grilling year-round means moisture and grease are working on your Weber's steel the whole time. Carbon traps moisture, moisture rusts metal, and an open cookbox vent left full of grease speeds all of it up.

Keep a cover on it — a real Weber cover that breathes, not a sealed plastic bag that traps condensation. Get the grease out before you store it for a cold stretch. And give it a real deep clean at least once a year, ideally heading into spring. A clean grill survives a Tri-State winter a lot better than a dirty one.

Every spring I get the same question from folks across Cincinnati and Northern Kentucky: my grill's looking rough, should I just buy a new one? Nine times out of ten, if it's a Weber, the answer is no. Restore it. Here's how I know.

## Webers are built to outlive the trend cycle

This is the thing people forget. A Weber Genesis isn't a disposable grill. The body is heavy-gauge steel, the burners and flavorizer bars are replaceable parts, and Weber still stocks components for models they stopped selling years ago. That's by design. These grills are meant to be maintained, not thrown out.

Most cheaper grills are riveted shut and built to last three or four seasons before the firebox rusts through. A Weber is built to be opened up, cleaned out, and put back to work. Restoring one isn't sentimental — it's just the smart math.

## How to tell if yours is worth it

Here's my honest checklist. Walk up to your grill and look for the things that actually matter:

- **The cookbox and lid.** Is the steel solid, or has it rusted into holes? Surface rust and baked-on carbon clean off. Rusted-through metal is the one thing that ends a grill.
- **The frame and cart.** Wobbly wheels and faded paint are cosmetic. A frame that's still square and sturdy means the grill's got a foundation worth building on.
- **The burners and bars.** These wear out, and that's fine — they're cheap, replaceable parts, not reasons to scrap the grill.

If the cookbox is solid and the frame is sound, your Weber is worth restoring. Everything else is just cleaning and parts.

## Restoring beats replacing on every count

A real deep clean plus a fresh set of flavorizer bars or burners — call it a fraction of the price of a new grill — gets you a Weber that cooks like new and lasts years longer. You keep the grill you already know how to use. You skip the assembly, the seasoning, the break-in. And you keep one more grill out of the landfill, which I'll admit I like more every year I do this.

The only time I'll tell you to replace it is when the cookbox has rusted through. Short of that, a Weber is almost always worth saving.
